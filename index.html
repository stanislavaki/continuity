    Imagine a block of granite of some 200 tonnes, freed from a quarry in Sardinia in the 1980s, carved into a work of art and shipped from Carrara around the coast of Europe through the Strait of Gibraltar, then carried up the Rhine and the Main, before finally being installed as a monumental infinity loop in the heart of the financial district of Frankfurt, Germany. At 80 tonnes, the finished sculpture has been called the largest granite monolith to be transported since the colossi of ancient Egypt.1 So what was the idea behind it, and what made it possible forty years ago?

    Conceived by the Swiss artist, architect and Bauhaus-trained polymath Max Bill, the sculpture was commissioned by Deutsche Bank in Frankfurt, Germany: the bank decided to approach Bill on 7 October 1981, and the contract was signed almost two years later.2 It was then realised over three years of continuous work by an international team of sculptors and stonemasons. Its production was led from a workshop in Carrara, Italy, by Dominique Stroobant, who directed the cutting, shipping and shaping of the granite and coordinated the considerable logistics the piece demanded. It belongs to a line of work that runs through Bill’s whole career: from 1935 on, in one experiment after another, he looked for ways to give infinity a form in space.3

    In Continuity, I attempted to realize an idea that had long been present in my mind: the representation of an infinite space in infinite motion.

    Max Bill, “Continuity and change”, 1953

    2

    The artist: Max Bill

    Max Bill (1908—1994) was a great Swiss polymath: architect, industrial and graphic designer, sculptor, painter — and also a prolific writer, scholar, and teacher.

    He attended the Bauhaus where he was taught by Josef Albers, László Moholy-Nagy, Paul Klee, Wassily Kandinsky and Oskar Schlemmer. Bill remained closely associated with the Bauhaus school and was a key figure in developing and propagating its principles — as a guest lecturer at the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich, and as a co-founder and first rector of the Ulm School of Design.

    The term concrete art was not Bill’s invention. It was coined in 1930 by the Dutch painter Theo van Doesburg, founder of De Stijl, in a manifesto published in the single issue of his journal Art Concret. The argument was deliberately contrary: a painting should not abstract from nature, because that leaves it forever secondary to the thing it was drawn from. A plane is a plane, a colour is a colour, and a work built from them is not an abstraction of reality but a concrete reality in its own right. Van Doesburg died the following year, and the idea might have gone with him.

    It was Bill who gave it a second life and a working method. In 1936, in the catalogue for an exhibition at the Kunsthaus Zürich, he set out his own definition: concrete design is design that arises of its own accord, following its own laws, without deriving them from natural phenomena or modelling itself on them. Where van Doesburg had written a polemic, Bill proposed a discipline — mathematics as the generative instrument rather than the decorative one. A progression, a rotation, a division of a surface could be stated as a rule and then simply carried out. This is the sense behind his remark that one can always understand his work, even without liking it: the rule is available for inspection.

    From there he built the movement an infrastructure. He organised the first international exhibition of concrete art in Basel in 1944, founded the bulletin abstrakt/konkret for Zurich’s Galerie des Eaux Vives, and took a teaching post at the Kunstgewerbeschule the same year — work that made the Zürcher Konkrete a school rather than a tendency. He carried the argument into industrial design through die gute Form, and into education as co-founder and first rector of the Ulm School of Design. Through his exhibitions and his contacts in Brazil and Argentina, the idea travelled to Latin America, where it produced movements of its own. Continuity belongs to that lineage: a form derived from a rule, made concrete in eighty tons of granite.

    3

    Kontinuität = an infinity loop?

    Max Bill was consistently inspired by mathematics and logic in art. His approach, which he called “verifiable logic,” guided him from early experimentation toward a precise method of making—and explaining—his work: “You can always understand my work, even if you don’t like it.” From the beginning of his career, Bill was fascinated by the Möbius strip. From his first attempts in 1936 to the realization of Continuity in Frankfurt, he produced dozens—if not hundreds—of variations on the infinity loop, exploring differences in scale, material, and environment.

    But how are we to understand the topology of Continuity? Is it a Möbius strip, a variation on an endless loop, or simply an artistic, sculptural interpretation of an infinite motif? In short, how did he do it? Even though Bill left some explanations, our own experiments and consultations with mathematicians did not yield an immediate understanding of how the sculpture was conceived and modeled.

    The interesting thing to note is that Bill thought he had invented a completely new shape. Even more curious was that he discovered it by twisting a strip of paper, just as Möbius had done many years previously. “Not long afterwards, people began to congratulate me on my fresh and original interpretation of the Egyptian symbol for infinity and the Möbius ribbon. I had never heard of either of them. My mathematical knowledge had never gone beyond routine architectural calculations, and I had no great interest in mathematics.”

    Barry Cipra (mathematician, Minnesota, USA): “Bill’s intuition that a single half twist connected to a double half twist should result in a single-sided, single-edge surface is quite correct; depending on the handedness of the twists, the loop you wind up with has either three twists or one. So it’s a mystery how he managed to add 1 and 2 and get 4.”

    By carefully examining the sculpture itself and creating a 3D model using 360° video and photography (fig. 1), and by comparing it with Bill’s metal sculpture from 1945–46—used by him as a 1:20 prototype (fig. 2)—we were able to analyze the work and conduct a series of spatial experiments. One of these resulted in an origami model that follows the original topology step by step and, surprisingly, resolves into a clear infinity-loop construction (fig. 3). Building on this, we developed a responsive interactive model that clearly demonstrates the topology of the Continuity. The result is striking: a pure mathematical model transforms into a work of art.

    In Continuity, I intended to achieve something similar: a single surface, a single boundary. Theoretically, a double helix, twisted twice by half a turn and fused with one twisted only half a turn, should have resulted in something akin to an ‘infinite loop,’ possessing only one surface and one boundary. For reasons unknown to me, this did not happen, so Continuity, despite the theoretically expected outcome, consists of two parallel bounded surfaces composed of infinitely many straight lines. This explanation may sound somewhat dry and simplistic, yet it is the only thing I can say about it myself.

    Max Bill

    The story of the making

    4.1

    Frankfurt, Germany

    1982

    Why would one of Germany's leading banks decide to place a large sculpture in front of its headquarters?

    As the Deutsche Bank Twin Towers rose in Frankfurt between 1979 and 1984, the bank's senior management faced a crucial question: what monumental artwork should stand at the threshold of the new headquarters? In 1981, while construction continued, the board began to search for a proper sculptor. Deutsche Bank had long committed to art and culture — a programme whose scope is evident today across the Towers' sixty floors, where the collection spans a hundred international artworks from more than forty countries.

    The choice had to meet several criteria. The artist should come from the German-speaking countries. The work should fit Deutsche Bank's collecting profile — meaning significant contemporary art. It should relate less to the building's facade than to the plaza in front of it. And the artist should be free in his solution, while that solution still reflected the functions of the bank.

    Few names fit better than Max Bill. An established Swiss artist and sculptor, modern and unexpected, his work corresponded closely to the bank's vision. Since his Bauhaus years, Bill had built an art of precise geometry, calculation and mathematics — carried through painting, sculpture and graphic work, an approach as innovative as it was rigorous.

    Bill first proposed a coloured column, conceived to present the viewer with numbers and quantities. Yet when the board asked how he himself would decide in their place, he gave his preference to Continuity — the work recommended by the bank's art advisers.

    The chance to finally bring this long-running hit, now approaching its fortieth anniversary, to life was so tempting that I cast aside all my reservations about its age — and a wave of almost insurmountable difficulties that was welling up inside me — and decided to take the plunge on this adventure.

    Max Bill

    Kontinuität worked through contrast to the straight Twin Towers while proposing harmony. The sculpture had once been destroyed by vandals in Zurich, and the idea of the unbroken continuity of world trade and banking — together with the restoration of art as a restoration of life in post-war Germany — suited the commission perfectly. The work was given to Max Bill.

    Carving the large granite sculpture took three years. It required a second sculptor, Dominique Stroobant, charged with its realisation alongside the bank's management. Stroobant approached the task with rigour. He set up the firm, hired assistants, found a suitable quarry for the granite in Sardinia, and built a workshop in the port of Carrara — working for three years always in consultation with, and under the supervision of, Max Bill.

    At haus bill — the home where Max Bill's widow Angela Thomas lives today — we found a large archive of documentation, accounts and invoices connected to the sculpture. Stroobant was responsible for the entire management: the extensive correspondence with Deutsche Bank over shipments, the selection and ordering of transport, the payment of invoices, the preparations for the opening, and the accompanying exhibition.

    It is precisely these documents — the notebooks, drawings, draft letters, expense calculations and photographs of the work in progress — that allow us today to understand the full complexity of realising such an ambitious idea. Stroobant did not simply make the sculpture: he coordinated an entire logistics operation, smoothed away bureaucratic obstacles, and ensured quality at every stage. Deutsche Bank, for its part, placed its trust in his experience and his sense of responsibility.

    Deutsche Bank's investment in art was never purely aesthetic. It reflected a broader conviction — that cultural engagement is part of what an institution owes to its people and to society, building cultural capital that extends from the workplace to the city and beyond. Kontinuität is perhaps the most eloquent expression of that idea: a sculpture whose name speaks to the unbroken continuity of this commitment.

    Max Bill's sculpture does not simply correspond to the bank building — it occupies an urbanistically significant setting, and will be seen and rediscovered daily by many people. Whether from a car, a park bench, on foot, from the Opernplatz or from the Taunusanlage: from every angle, and for everyone who takes pleasure in it, this granite sculpture with its infinite loop is meant to be an offering, a point of reference.

    Herbert Zapp, Deutsche Bank board member and initiator of its art collection, at the sculpture's inauguration in 1986.

    The search for the stone began before there was anyone to cut it. On 25 April 1983 the “Esagono” work collective was formed — the group of sculptors and technicians who would spend the next three years on a single object. Five months later, on 13 September, Max Bill travelled to Sardinia with Rodolfo Costantino to inspect the Scarraciana quarry in the island’s north-east, looking for a block large enough, and sound enough, to survive what was about to be done to it.

    They found it there. On 20 October 1983 the charges were set and a block of some 500 tons was blasted clear of the rock face — many times the weight of the finished sculpture, because the finished sculpture had to come out of a single uninterrupted mass. Nothing about Continuity could be assembled from parts. Every curve had to already be present inside the stone, waiting to be found.

    Through October and November the block was worked down where it lay. Five hundred tons became three hundred; three hundred became 180, and the difference was left on the quarry floor as waste. What remained was not yet a sculpture but a polyhedron — a faceted, roughly geometric solid, shaped as much for the journey ahead as for the carving still to come. In that form it was loaded onto a Fagioli truck with a 52-metre platform, a vehicle built for loads that ordinary roads are not.

    The reductions were not a matter of tidying the block up. They were the condition of it moving at all: at five hundred tons nothing on the island could lift it, carry it, or float it, and each stage of cutting bought a margin against the limits of the crane, the platform and the ship that would have to take it in turn. What was struck off in Sardinia was never going to be part of the sculpture, but the sculpture could not have left without it going first.

    Which was the next problem, because no road ran from the quarry to the highway that could carry such a weight. So one was built: a temporary road laid across open ground for the sake of a single vehicle making a single trip, then abandoned. It is the kind of detail that measures the ambition of the commission more precisely than any statement about it — an entire road, built once, to move one stone.

    On 8 and 9 February 1984 the public route was closed for two days while the truck carried the block across Sardinia to the port of San Teresa di Gallura, travelling at the pace such a load demands. For those two days the island’s traffic arranged itself around the granite rather than the other way round.

    This was only the first leg. Ahead lay a route that would run some 2,800 kilometres by water and 1,200 by road, across five countries and most of six months, before the stone reached Frankfurt. The crossing to the mainland was the short part of it, and even that had to wait on the sea.

    At the port the weather turned against it. The cargo waited ten days for conditions safe enough to sail — a hundred and eighty tons of granite standing at a quayside while the Tyrrhenian stayed unworkable. Then, on 20 February 1984, the ship reached Marina di Carrara on the Tuscan coast, where the Esagono workshop was waiting. The stone had left Sardinia as raw material; it arrived at the place where three years of carving would begin.

    4.3

    Carrara, workshop

    1983–1986

    A site near the port was chosen for convenient stone transport and waste handling — roughly 120 tons of granite would have to be removed. A temporary metal building rose around the block, with a separate pavilion housing the four-fold magnified plaster model that served as the master measurement.

    Work began in February 1984. A grid of “measuring points” was carried across from that enlarged gypsum model using giant dividers, so that every dimension cut into the granite could be referred back to a single authority.

    The cutting was done with drilling machines and diamond chains made specially for the job — ordinary ones are built for marble, not granite. Most of the work happened inside the block, by methods both intricate and dangerous: only two people could be within the stone at a time, so the carving ran in shifts, and the long internal channels that the loop’s hidden topology depends on were opened out gradually.

    By January 1985 the main through-cuts were complete and the interior shape had begun to emerge. On 22 March 1986 the sculpture was turned over onto three support points so that carving could continue across the faces it had been resting on. It was finished on 30 June 1986.

    4.4

    Transport: Gibraltar → Rotterdam

    1986

    Between 4 and 8 August 1986, the finished Continuity was mounted into a sturdy steel transport frame in Carrara and loaded onto the cargo ship MV Kupari.

    The Kupari sailed via the Strait of Gibraltar, west of Portugal, across the Bay of Biscay and through the English Channel to the North Sea — a 2,800 km open-water passage taking just over two weeks.

    4.5

    Rotterdam → Frankfurt

    1986

    After unloading in Rotterdam, the sculpture was transferred to a river barge and pushed up the Rhine and Main rivers toward the German interior.

    4.6

    Frankfurt

    1986 / 2026

    In Frankfurt, a Colonia crane moved from the Osthafen to Taunusanlage 12 lifted the 80-tonne sculpture above the trees and into place beside the Deutsche Bank towers the following morning.
